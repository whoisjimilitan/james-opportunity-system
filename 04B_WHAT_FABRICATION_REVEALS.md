# 04B — What Fabrication Reveals: Anti-Patterns of Weak Discovery

This document is not a checklist. It's a guide to recognizing when thinking is sloppy.

When you see these patterns, stop. The discovery isn't ready.

---

## Anti-Pattern 1: The Invented Benefit

**What it sounds like:**
"They're expanding into West Africa, so they probably need help with training delivery."

**Why it's weak:**
- No evidence they've identified this need
- "Probably" means it's assumed, not researched
- The benefit is hypothetical, not verified

**What gives it away:**
- Words like "probably," "likely," "could be," "might need"
- No evidence from their actual communications or structure
- You're inferring what they want, not discovering what they've signaled

**The honest version:**
"They're expanding into West Africa. Their business model requires local facilitators. We don't yet know if facilitator preparation is a problem for them, but it's a hypothesis worth testing."

---

## Anti-Pattern 2: The Forced Counterfactual

**What it sounds like:**
"If James is removed, they would lose access to his crisis training expertise."

**Why it's weak:**
- They don't currently have him
- They're not losing anything that exists
- You're inventing what would be different

**What gives it away:**
- The counterfactual works only if you imagine adding James to a situation that currently doesn't include him
- You can't answer "what do they currently lose by not having him?" because the answer is "nothing—he's not there"

**The honest version:**
"If James takes on training-of-trainers role, facilitators get 3 days of real training instead of being handed materials. That's the difference. Without him, they handle it differently (maybe themselves, maybe they skip it, maybe they fail)."

**The counterfactual only works if James is solving something that currently exists or is currently happening.**

## Anti-Pattern 3: The Imaginary Organization

**What it sounds like:**
"A UK training platform wants to expand into West Africa and needs local partners."

**Why it's weak:**
- You're stating what you inferred
- You're not showing the evidence
- The reader can't verify if the need is real

**What gives it away:**
- When you strip away the source evidence, the claim collapses
- You can't point to a specific signal (announcement, hiring, report) that proves it
- You're treating inference as fact

**The honest version:**
"A UK training platform announced expansion plans, hired a Regional Director for Africa, and published a case study of a Ghana partnership. [Source: their annual report, company announcement, case study link]. What this signals: they're investing real money. What's uncertain: whether they've identified specific capability gaps yet."

---

## Anti-Pattern 4: The Vague Arrangement

**What it sounds like:**
"James could help them with their training efforts in West Africa."

**Why it's weak:**
- "Help with training efforts" is so vague it applies to almost anyone
- No one is going to respond to that
- You haven't actually figured out what James would do

**What gives it away:**
- When you ask "what would James actually do?" you get a shrug
- The arrangement could fit a hundred other people just as well
- There's no reason it has to be James

**The honest version:**
"James would prepare their local facilitators for delivery through a structured 3-day training before they go live. This matters because facilitator preparation is the difference between 40% and 80% successful implementations based on their historical data."

---

## Anti-Pattern 5: The Invisible Economics

**What it sounds like:**
"This could be a great opportunity for James."

**Why it's weak:**
- No one has thought about who pays, how much, or when
- "Great opportunity" is emotion, not analysis
- You don't know if money is actually involved

**What gives it away:**
- When asked "who pays James and why?" you get "well, they would probably..."
- When asked "how much?" you guess without evidence
- When asked "is this even a paid arrangement?" you realize you don't know

**The honest version:**
"This arrangement would be 6 months at €20-25K based on market rates for training coordination in West Africa [source: comparable roles in similar organizations]. The organization has expansion budget. Payment would likely be a monthly retainer. James's cost would be roughly €5K (for travel/accommodation), leaving net income of €15-20K over 6 months, or €2.5-3.3K per month."

## Anti-Pattern 6: The Self-Serving Signal

**What it sounds like:**
"They're looking for partners, so James could be a partner."

**Why it's weak:**
- "Looking for partners" is so generic it could mean anything
- You're matching a vague organizational statement to James
- No actual opportunity here yet, just overlap of language

**What gives it away:**
- When you remove James's name, the signal still applies to a thousand other people
- The opportunity isn't specific to James; it's generic to the category
- You're fitting James into a pre-existing category instead of discovering his specific value

**The honest version:**
"They're expanding into Ghana. Their model requires local expertise in both their content area AND understanding trauma-informed practice in West African educational settings. James has both. Most facilitators have one or the other."

---

## Anti-Pattern 7: The Orphaned Development Pathway

**What it sounds like:**
"James could get certified in X, and then he could be a trainer for Y."

**Why it's weak:**
- No evidence anyone actually needs Y
- The certification path is hypothetical
- You're building a future for a need that might not exist

**What gives it away:**
- When you ask "who would hire someone with this certification?" you realize you don't know
- When you ask "is there actual demand for this?" you're guessing
- The pathway is invented, not discovered

**The honest version:**
"An organization is launching a crisis intervention certification program in West Africa. They need instructors. James could credibly become an instructor. [Evidence: the program exists, enrollment is 300+, they've posted for instructors, they require experience that James has]. If he takes the 6-week instructor training, he'd be qualified by Q2 and there would likely be contracts available."

---

## Anti-Pattern 8: The Forced Enthusiasm

**What it sounds like:**
"This is an incredible opportunity that James absolutely should pursue!"

**Why it's weak:**
- You're making the decision for James
- Enthusiasm is covering up that the opportunity might not be real
- You're trying to make something work that doesn't

**What gives it away:**
- Exclamation marks and superlatives masking uncertainty
- When the actual facts are stripped away, nothing's there
- You're selling the opportunity instead of describing it

**The honest version:**
"This arrangement has three strengths [list them] and one real gap [state it]. Whether it's worth James's time depends on [specific factor]. Here's what we'd need to learn to be more confident."

---

## What Honest Discovery Sounds Like

- Specific evidence, not general claims
- Uncertainty is explicit, not hidden
- The counterfactual is clear and testable
- The arrangement is specific enough that only James (or a small category) fits
- Economics are researched, not guessed
- The need is discovered, not assumed
- You can separate fact from inference
- You can explain exactly what would disprove it

When you catch yourself in an anti-pattern, stop. Go back to research. Find the actual signal, not the imagined one.
